Left Hanging Behind

Nothing is more seductive for man than his freedom of conscience, but nothing is a greater cause of suffering.

short-story
Published

November 3, 2026

The ceiling light glares harshly, drowning the room in an apathetic, sickening white. Under its oppressive beam, histhe young man finishes his scribblings on a paper. The handwriting is neat and meticulous - certainly unusual for him. He stacks the papers, and alligns them perfectly on the desk. A long, heavy sigh escapes him - a release. Walking slowly to the window, he stands, watching the city.

The man was twenty-six years old.

He has been standing at the window for some time and frankly, he could not have told you how long if asked. Time has been acting weird lately - it moves untimely. A clock always ticks and tocks. It’s an unwritten law of time that it is constant and always continuing. But not his time - his time is elastic. It stretches and compresses until hours become minutes and minutes become hours. It all melts and fizzes up, becoming incomprehensible until it disappears altogether and he surfaces from it the way you surface from shallow water, gasping for air and unsure of how long he was under.

He used to love this town. Everything used to be so new. New job. New faces. New friends.

The street is doing what streets normally do. A woman walks with a stroller. A teenager on his phone, his eyes glued to it as he crosses the road. A kid holding her mother’s hand, both smiling and talking. All of them going somewhere, and mattering, to another or themselves. He observes them with a scientific curiousity - the way they move through the world as if the world is simply there, as if its continued existence is a thing you can rely on - no maintenance, no faith. He used to do that, taking it all for granted. He can’t remember when he stopped.

He had just moved into the apartment some years ago. He loved his apartment.

The apartment is clean. It’s not like it was dirty or messy before, but it was simply never this clean. He woke up this morning and cleaned it with a thoroughness that even surprised him - it was alike his usual cursory pass but more deliberate. The dishes, the desk; all tidied up. The towels and jackets hung properly on the rack for once, not just draped across. The bed was even remade and the corners smoothed and tightened. The room is fragrant; there is a hint of rosewood in the air, by virtue of the incense a friend had gotten him. He loves it, a lot, and wonders to himself why he had saved it all this time. His room is sparkling, so meticulously tidied with a care that he hasn’t paid it in months or perhaps years. He doesn’t ask himself why he is doing this now. He doesn’t care, or rather he doesn’t want to answer.

The incense was a gift from someone he cared about. The first gift he ever received from them. He couldn’t bring himself to use it because using it meant losing it - and he had so little left of them to lose. He needed it. To remind himself of happier times. To remember happiness.

His phone is on the desk. On it, there are three unsent messages. Who are they addressed to? It doesn’t matter. He just needs someone to read them. He wants -

He won’t let his mind do this to him again. He looked at the messages for a long time this morning and had turned the phone face down and not touched it since. They don’t say what he wants to say. He doesn’t know what he wants them to say. He doesn’t even know if language is sufficient for what he needs it to do anymore. His heart races. It is always like this now - restless like a bird throwing itself against a cage. He can’t recall the last time it was quiet.

He was sleep-deprived, but it was not his fault. The nightmares left him no other option.

He was eating too little too. He had no appetite. He couldn’t have had an appetite. But he did eat half a toast this morning and he smiled at the thought of it. It was an accomplishment to be celebrated.

He finally turns from the window. Something caught on the edges; something that is in his hand. He’s been holding onto it, turning it over, fiddling with it, without registering that he’s been doing it. His fingers have been at it the way fingers work at worry beads or rosaries, a nervous unconscious industry. He looks down at it and then looks away quickly, at the middle distance, at nothing. He doesn’t want to see it; he doesn’t want to contemplate it; he doesn’t want to acknowledge it.

Again, his heart is doing something irregular. No, not painful. Just so dull, just - present in a way that hearts usually aren’t, so unyielding in announcing itself, so demanding in being noticed. But it’s impossible. Its master is too weak, too docile, too scared. No one can know. He offers a silent apology to his heart.

Too much throbbing. The dullness pounded harder and harder. It wanted to be heard. No, it must be heard.

He sits down on the edge of the bed. He breathes. He gets up, shuffles awkwardly, then sits down again.

The light through the curtains is at an angle it always gets to in the late afternoon. He knows this light but he has only recently come to know this specific quality at this intensity. It is a beautiful light, the way it spreads across the floor, makes ordinary things briefly more than themselves and warms the room just right. It is ephemeral but cosmically incomprehensible. A ball of fire, millions of miles away, and yet its heat finds his skin. He has always loved this light without ever thinking to say so. This seems important suddenly. He doesn’t understand why.

The thing in his hands was not complicated by any means. Yet, it took him longer than it should have. Was it indecisiveness or a silent pleading that perhaps something would change? His hands kept stopping, undoing it, starting over. The fingers wouldn’t cooperate. Something in his body’s fine motor system was staging a quiet resistance - involuntary and futile. He continued anyway.

He is tired. That’s the truest thing he knows about himself since last year - the bone-deep exhaustion of being himself, of carrying the weight of his own continuing presence in the world, of not knowing what’s next, of waking up every morning and finding it still there, all of it, nothing changed, of having to slog through everything again, of having to play a role that he can never fill. He is tired of the effort. Tired of pretending that the effort is normal. It is not humanly possible, he thinks. He doesn’t understand how someone can live like this and act as if it’s fine.

He sighs. A bad habit of his that he picked up not very long ago.

He thought to himself that he was wasting too much time. He started to grow restless.

He gets up and walks to the closet to pick up a stool. He shouldn’t mess up the bed any longer. The closet door sticks out slightly, as it always has, in the way he has promised to fix but never did. He pulls it open. He always keeps a stool for when visitors come. There have been fewer of those lately. He reaches for it. Stops. What stopped him wasn’t the stool. It was a Bible. Not dramatically - it didn’t fall on him, didn’t catch the light in some cinematic fashion. It’s just there, sitting on the stool, slightly crooked and caked with a layer of dust. Why is it here? When was the last time he opened it? Didn’t his faith define him in the past? Didn’t God mean something to him once? He doesn’t remember how it all changed.

He is not a praying man anymore. He’s not sure when he stopped either - it belongs to the same period as everything else he lost track of - lost to the torpid attrition of the things that once held him together. Yes, he once knew this book.

though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me.

The verse surfaces without permission. He didn’t reach for it - it reached for him. He can see the room where he read it. A Saturday afternoon, someone’s living room, sitting together on the floor because there weren’t enough chairs. He sees the smiles of others - he doesn’t remember who. Hasn’t seen them and never bothered to contact. He was laughing at something with them - he doesn’t remember what. But he would not forget the laughter; and the experience of being there in the room with those people on that afternoon with no reason to be anywhere else.

He stands there by the stool, the verse sinking itself into his mind - though I walk through the valley - and what strikes him, standing here now, is the walking. Not the fear, not the comfort, not the rod nor the staff. The walking. That whoever wrote it was still moving. Still putting one foot in front of the other through the worst of it, through the valley and its shadow. He takes the Bible and the stool out.

He doesn’t know why he grabbed the Bible. Nonetheless, he leaves it on his table.

For some reason, his hands have stopped trembling.

That’s new. They were trembling all morning, through the cleaning, through the messages he didn’t send, through the sitting down and getting up and then sitting down - and now they’ve stopped, and in their steadiness there is something that feels almost like peace. He is still terrified, of course, but now assured.

He looked around the room.

He looks around the room.

The coffee mug with its ring on the wood. The books that he had painstakingly collected. His piano and all his other instruments that he had stopped playing. The phone face-down on the desk, those three messages still unsent. These things will continue like this after. They will be here exactly like this but he will not be here to see them.

The light at this hour, especially.

He looked at the light for a long time.

He looks at the light for a long time. A very long time. He would engrave it onto his retina if he could.
